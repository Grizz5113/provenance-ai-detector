The smell of old paper and binding glue is, without exaggeration, my favorite scent in the world. It’s a quiet aroma, a comforting one that speaks of accumulated knowledge and patient craftsmanship. This isn't some nostalgic fondness for libraries I developed as a child; it’s rooted in an obsession with cartography, specifically, the creation and evolution of maps throughout history. 

It started, oddly enough, with a faded, topographical map of the Scottish Highlands I found tucked away in my grandfather’s attic. It wasn't a pristine, modern rendering – far from it. The paper was brittle, yellowed by time and exposed to countless sunlit days. Lines were drawn in ink that had bled slightly into the fibers, creating an almost watercolor effect. The scale was off, the symbols archaic, and the coastline utterly unrecognizable compared to what exists today. Yet, there was a raw power to it, a tangible record of a landscape perceived and interpreted through the eyes of someone living within it centuries ago.

I began devouring everything I could find about mapmaking – not just the technical aspects of surveying and projection, but also the stories embedded within each creation. I discovered that maps aren't simply objective representations of reality; they’re inherently subjective, shaped by the biases and priorities of their creators. Early medieval maps, for instance, weren't concerned with accuracy in a modern sense. Jerusalem, according to the mappa mundi I studied, was located at the center of the world, surrounded by oceans that encircled Europe, Asia, and Africa. This wasn’t a mistake; it reflected the religious beliefs and worldview of its creator, a monk seeking to illustrate God's dominion. 

The more I learned, the more fascinating I found the shift in mapping methodologies over time. The Renaissance saw an explosion of exploration, fueled by a desire for trade and empire, which led to increasingly detailed and geographically accurate maps – though even these were still tied to specific agendas, often reflecting European perspectives and colonial ambitions. Then came the age of scientific cartography, where mathematics and precise instruments began to dominate, pushing accuracy to new levels while simultaneously stripping away some of the romanticized narratives that had previously colored mapmaking. 

I became particularly captivated by the concept of “lost maps” – charts abandoned or destroyed, representing geographical knowledge that has been irrevocably erased. The disappearance of the Strait of Magellan from Spanish maps in the early sixteenth century, driven by a desire to conceal its existence from the Portuguese, highlights how cartography can be used as a tool of power and control.  It’s an incredibly complex field, blending science, history, art, and even politics. 

I want to understand *why* people create maps – what motivates them to painstakingly chart the world around them? What stories do they intend to tell, or perhaps, inadvertently reveal? I'm interested in how a map reflects not just the physical landscape, but also the cultural, social, and political context of its creation.  I want to explore how different cultures have approached the challenge of representing space, and how these approaches have shaped our understanding of the world.

My interest isn’t simply academic; it feels deeply connected to a fundamental human impulse – the desire to understand and navigate our surroundings. I see mapmaking as a form of storytelling, a way of making sense of the unknown.  I believe that studying this field will provide me with a unique perspective on history, geography, and even the nature of knowledge itself. The possibility of uncovering lost stories hidden within faded lines and antiquated symbols—of contributing to our understanding of how we’ve shaped and been shaped by the world around us – is an incredibly compelling prospect, one that I feel compelled to pursue with earnest curiosity and a deep appreciation for the quiet beauty of old paper.
