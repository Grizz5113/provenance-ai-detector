The 14th Amendment to the United States Constitution was adopted in 1868, after the Civil War. It was designed to protect the rights of newly freed slaves and to ensure that they were treated equally under the law. One of the key provisions of the 14th Amendment is the "due process" clause, which says that no person shall be "deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." This means that the government must follow certain procedures before it can take away someone's life, freedom, or property.

In the late 1800s, some people argued that the due process clause should also apply to corporations, which are legal entities that are created by the state. They argued that corporations should have the same constitutional protections as individuals because they are made up of people and because they play an important role in the economy.

The Supreme Court ultimately agreed with this argument and ruled that corporations are entitled to certain constitutional rights, including the right to due process and the right to free speech. This means that the government cannot take away a corporation's property or rights without following certain procedures, and it also means that corporations have the right to express their views and opinions on political issues.

Thomas Jefferson, who was one of the Founding Fathers of the United States, did not live to see the 14th Amendment or the Supreme Court's decision to grant constitutional rights to corporations. However, he did believe in the importance of individual rights and limited government, and it is possible that he might have disagreed with the idea of granting constitutional rights to corporations.
