I firmly believe that a national popular vote should determine the outcome of presidential elections in the United States. The current system, relying on the Electoral College, utilizes a slate of electors chosen by each party to ultimately elect the president – an arrangement predicated on the assumption that these electors will faithfully support their party’s nominee. However, this reliance on pledged individuals introduces significant vulnerabilities; despite efforts to maintain loyalty, the trust placed in these electors is not always guaranteed.

Frankly, I find the Electoral College system deeply problematic and would advocate for a shift towards a national popular vote. Considering recent trends, including the potential for another closely contested election where the popular vote winner could be denied the presidency – as we’ve seen repeatedly – it becomes increasingly clear that the Electoral College is an outdated mechanism ill-suited to modern democratic principles. My argument centers on the fundamental principle of majority rule: the candidate receiving the most votes nationwide should, by definition, hold the office of president.

One particularly concerning aspect of the Electoral College is the possibility of a tie, which would then trigger a vote in the House of Representatives, with state delegations casting their ballots for the president. As highlighted in the provided text, this process raises serious questions about whether the outcome truly reflects the will of the people, given that voters often cast different votes for presidential and congressional candidates. A national popular vote presents far fewer complications and drawbacks than the Electoral College; it offers a more direct and transparent expression of the electorate’s preference.
