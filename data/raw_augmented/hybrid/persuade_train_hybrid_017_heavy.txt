The Electoral College remains a deeply embedded feature of our constitutional framework, having shaped presidential selections since its inception alongside the Constitution itself. The question of its continued relevance is frequently raised: does it persist simply because we inhabit an era of constant innovation and improvement, or does it genuinely fail to reflect the will of the American people? Commonly viewed as an outdated and undemocratic mechanism for choosing a president, the Electoral College warrants critical examination. 

Consider, for instance, the potential for unforeseen circumstances – a scenario mirroring past instances of misaligned voting—where a candidate gains the presidency through means that don’t accurately represent popular support. Alternatively, what would occur in the event of an electoral tie? The Constitution stipulates that such situations would trigger a vote within the House of Representatives, with state delegations casting individual votes rather than individual representatives. This system is particularly problematic because each state receives only one vote, regardless of its population; for example, the single representative from Wyoming effectively represents the voting preferences of approximately 500,000 citizens. Critically, the Electoral College avoids the necessity of conducting runoff elections, thereby preventing any candidate from achieving a clear majority of the popular votes cast nationwide.
